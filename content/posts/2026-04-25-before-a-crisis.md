The big question that has been on people’s mind at this point in time is: why hasn’t the market crashed yet?

Yes, there are plenty of signs of technical corrections, but we haven’t seen a 20% or 50% downturn of a major market index. By comparing this downturn with the S&P 500 during 2008 or the dot-com crash, it’s really been a faster and milder correction — not the 5-6x recession we’ve seen before.

It’s “Irrational Exuberance,” if I may quote Greenspan.

There are also signs of Trump doing market manipulation in plain sight. We all feel a crisis is coming, but how?

## The structural cracks

There are a few structural risks in the U.S. economy at this moment, notably the sovereign debt. The U.S. sovereign debt is a bit more than 100% debt-to-GDP ratio, but that ratio isn’t usually the best metric to tell if an economy is under pressure. As an example, China’s sovereign debt has 3x the ratio of their own GDP, but most of their debt is held by local governments, so it’s less of an external pressure. The U.S. sovereign debt is largely held by other countries — Japan, China, Canada, Belgium — and there’s already a tendency to sell U.S. treasuries to escape the dollar system. Many countries have been reducing holdings of U.S. dollars and buying into gold for the past few years.

However, exactly what does a U.S. debasement mean for regular individual investors?

To me, it boils down to buying power. In other words, how much you can buy with one dollar, measured against a global standard — how much you need to buy an okay meal, say a McDonald’s burger or a local budget fast-food option. In the current standard, if we compare the U.S. and China:

| Category | United States | China (USD equiv.) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Budget / value burger** | $3 – $5 | $0.43 – $2.31 |
| **Standard fast food burger** | $6 – $9 | $2.00 – $4.70 |
| **Premium fast food / fast casual** | $11 – $13 | $5.50 (Shake Shack) |
| **Full combo meal** | $10 – $18 | $4.34 – $5.93 |
| **Median restaurant burger** | **$14.64** | ~$3 – $5 (fast food avg.) |

This roughly translates to 3x buying power of CNY in the current setting. Why do we have this in the first place? Part of the reason is the Petrodollar system, where oil — a fundamental material of modern economy — is priced in USD.

## The Treasury’s quiet panic

There’s another piece of the debt picture that I think deserves attention: the U.S. Treasury has been buying back its own bonds. $15 billion on April 21, 2026 alone, and the buybacks have been getting more frequent and larger. At first glance this seems contradictory — why would the government buy back debt it already issued?

Part of it is a liquidity problem. Older bonds (the industry calls them “off-the-run”) are like used cars on a lot. A bond from 2019 paying 2% looks terrible when new bonds pay 4.5%. Nobody wants it at face value, so it sits on a dealer’s balance sheet collecting dust — fewer buyers, wider spreads, harder to move without eating a loss. The Treasury buys these back to keep the market plumbing functional.

But the more concerning part is the maturity management. The government is retiring older long-term bonds and reissuing shorter-term ones. I think of it as refinancing a 30-year mortgage with a credit card. Your monthly payment looks better on paper, but you’re rolling the dice every three months on what rate you’ll get when you have to reissue that same trillion dollars.

Why does the government do this? It’s not really strategic — it’s desperation math. A 30-year bond at 4.8% costs $48 billion a year, but you know exactly what you owe for decades. A 3-month T-bill at 4.2% is cheaper *today*, but you have to reissue the full amount every quarter at whatever rate the market demands. The government shifts short because short-term rates have been slightly lower, it makes this year’s interest expense look less terrifying, and money market funds reliably buy T-bills. It’s the national equivalent of chasing a 0% intro APR and hoping you’ll figure it out before the rate resets.

A sign of the USD decline has been out in stark daylight since 2024, from the [Treasury debt buyback program](https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov/datasets/treasury-securities-buybacks/buybacks-operations), where frequencies of debt buyback went alive and wild.

## How bond yields actually work

Here’s something I didn’t understand for a while: when people say “the 10-year yield spiked,” it sounds like bonds just became more attractive. More yield, more return, right? But it works the opposite way. A yield spike means investors are *selling* — dumping bonds, driving prices down, and effectively demanding more compensation to hold U.S. debt.

I kept confusing yield and interest rate, so let me clarify (mostly for my own sanity). When a bond is issued, it has a fixed coupon rate — say 4% on a $1,000 bond, paying $40 a year. That never changes. But the bond trades on secondary markets. If confidence drops and the price falls to $800, a new buyer still gets $40 a year — but on an $800 investment, that’s a 5% yield. The coupon didn’t change. The yield rose because the price fell. The Fed’s “interest rate” (the federal funds rate) is a completely separate lever — an overnight rate banks charge each other, which influences bond yields but doesn’t set them.

So when Congress proposes a fiscally loose budget and the 10-year yield jumps 50 basis points in a week, that’s the bond market saying “we don’t trust your fiscal path.” Higher yields immediately raise borrowing costs for everything — government debt, mortgages, corporate loans — so it forces policymakers to respond. That’s the “discipline.”

The catch: it only works if politicians actually listen to the signal.

## Who’s buying and selling

Four groups, roughly.

1. Foreign governments — Japan, China, UK — holding bonds as reserve assets. This is the group that’s been shrinking as de-dollarization accelerates.
2. Domestic institutions like pension funds and insurance companies, who need long-duration assets to match their long-term liabilities (they’re paying retirees in 20 years, so they need predictable returns over 20 years).
3. The Fed, which bought bonds aggressively during quantitative easing but has been doing the opposite lately.
4. And individual investors and funds, who jump in when yields look attractive enough.

When the first group sells and the third stops buying, the remaining buyers only step up at higher yields. That’s the mechanism.

Side note: of course a question to note here — why the heck is the Fed doing QT while the Treasury is doing “QE” by buying back debts (without printing real money)? I deeply suspect a heated Treasury v. Fed fight is ongoing, and the crime probe into Powell was just a hint.

## The government’s options (all bad)

What can the government do about any of this? The options are limited and mostly bad. Cut spending — politically difficult. Raise taxes — politically difficult. Issue more debt to pay interest on existing debt — which is what’s actually happening, and that’s a debt spiral. Or pressure the Fed to cut rates (which Trump has done publicly), which itself undermines confidence in the system.

U.S. interest payments now exceed defense spending. That’s when abstract debt stops being abstract.

## The middle class squeeze

I worry about the American middle class over the next 5-10 years. I’m talking about households making roughly $60K to $150K — teachers, nurses, accountants, paralegals, junior managers. They’re heading into what I think of as a squeeze from above and below simultaneously.

From above: housing, healthcare, and education costs have been compounding at 5-8% annually while median wages grow 3-4%. That gap compounds quietly until suddenly you can’t afford the life your parents had at your age.

From below: AI and automation are coming for white-collar jobs in exactly this income bracket. These jobs are skilled enough to be expensive but routine enough to be automatable. Below $60K, work tends to be physical (harder to automate). Above $150K, work tends to be strategic and relationship-driven (harder to replace). The middle gets compressed from both ends.

And from both sides at once: dollar debasement and tariffs erode purchasing power. Tariffs are effectively a consumption tax — they make imported goods more expensive, which directly reduces what your dollar can buy. This connects right back to the burger table above. If you’re already struggling to keep up with 5% annual cost increases on a 3% raise, adding tariff-driven inflation on top makes the math ugly.

The macro chain that concerns me goes something like this: job displacement reduces consumer spending (which is 70% of U.S. GDP), which drags corporate earnings down, which triggers equity corrections. Unlike previous recessions, I don’t think the middle class can just “wait it out” this time — the assets they own (homes, 401(k)s) lose relative value while costs keep climbing. That’s a fundamentally different kind of downturn.
