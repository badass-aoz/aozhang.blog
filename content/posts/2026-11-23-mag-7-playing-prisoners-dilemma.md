## Prologue: Being Right for the Wrong Reason

Last year, I fell into the most seductive trap in investing: mistaking luck for skill.

I was (and still am) a big fan of Ray Dalio, who has been warning about countries moving away from the U.S. dollar and global trade shifts, hence I bought heavily into an international stock index fund (`FZILX`). When the fund surged spectacularly before the US-Iran War, I congratulated myself for making the right macro forecasting.

Except it was a total illusion. 

The fund hadn't rallied because of global trade rebalancing; it rallied because its biggest holding, TSMC, builds the world's most advanced AI chips—and five Silicon Valley giants were pouring hundreds of billions of dollars into AI hardware. 

I hadn't predicted the global economy. I had accidentally bought a ticket on the AI spending train.

There's a cost—most of all, spending hours watching dramatic geopolitical doom videos that deliver chronic stress and zero useful investing decisions (sure there's dopamine so I sometimes treat it as a Korean drama replacement). 

What's more useful, generally, is the balance sheet.

---

## The Trap: An Insurance Policy That Never Expires

Imagine you run a trillion-dollar tech company in 2026. You face two choices for **capital spending** (the money spent on data centers, power grids, and AI chips):

* **Option A (Stay Disciplined)**: Cut AI hardware spending in half, protect profit margins, and let your **free cash flow** (the leftover cash a business actually keeps after paying its bills and buying equipment) surge.
* **Option B (Keep Spending)**: Keep pouring billions into giant server clusters, knowing full well that today's AI software sales don't come close to paying for them—simply because every rival is doing the exact same thing.

Look across the Magnificent Seven today and you can watch the board split in real time. Four giants—**Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta**—chose Option B, pouring over **$600 billion combined into 2026 capital spending**, while **Apple** quietly sat out and **Nvidia** acted as the dealer (aka: banker) at the center of the table.

The big spenders aren't crazy; this is the textbook Prisoner's Dilemma in Game Theory, where two suspects are questioned in separate rooms, and neither knows what the other will say:

|                     | Partner Stays Silent                                                 | Partner Betrays You                                                                 |
| :------------------ | :------------------------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **You Stay Silent** | *Best collective outcome*: 1 year in prison each.                    | *Worst individual outcome*: Partner walks free; **you get 10 years**.               |
| **You Betray**      | *Best individual outcome*: **You walk free**; partner gets 10 years. | *Nash Equilibrium*: **3 years each**—worse for both, yet neither dares stay silent. |

Swap prison sentences for corporate survival, and Big Tech faces the exact same payoff table:

|                       | Rivals Cut Spending                                           | Rivals Keep Spending                                                                    |
| :-------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------ | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **You Cut Spending**  | *Peace*: Everyone saves cash and grows steadily.              | **Wiped Out**: If a breakthrough arrives, you become the next Nokia or Kodak overnight. |
| **You Keep Spending** | **Winner-Take-All**: You control the next computing platform. | *Stalemate (Today)*: $600B spent; leftover cash shrinks; nobody dies yet.               |

> *"Even if Meta wastes $200 billion, it is worth it. Going too slow means you are out of the game."* — Mark Zuckerberg

One of the most overlooked risks is the commitment, because AI spending is never a one-off expense—it has to be renewed every year. $600B this year, $800B next year, and possibly $1T by 2028. Nor can CEOs back off: Wall Street punishes any unilateral spending cut as a white flag, and the White House views AI computing power as a national-security race that it can't lose.

It's equivalent to **an annual insurance premium on corporate survival with no expiration date**, as nobody knows when—or if—human-level AI will arrive.

### The Accounting Mirage Hiding the Cash Drain

I'm in Information Security, but I have been saying that CFO is the biggest hacker in a tech company, because fundamentally, it's about how you can get around rules (and hopefully, how you can prevent that). In a finance sense, it's about making the spending look lighter than it really is. There are three main techniques:

* **Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV, aka "shell company")**: separate entities funded by private lenders to keep massive data-center construction leases off the main balance sheet.
* **Off-Book Commitments & Amortization**: multi-year "take-or-pay" electricity contracts stay off the balance sheet as unrecorded future commitments until the facility turns on, while upfront grid and infrastructure costs are amortized over 4–5 years instead of hitting upfront.
* **Depreciation**: GPUs in particular practically depreciate in 3 years thanks to fast chip iteration (if in 18 months you can get a new GPU for the same price with much higher efficiency, why would anyone want the current version?), yet the accounting depreciation cost is stretched out over a 5-year lifespan.

For finance noobs like myself, I need to look at the **True Leftover Cash**:

**True Leftover Cash** = Cash Coming In − Direct Equipment Spending − Hidden Lease Payments − Locked-In Electricity Bills

Software companies used to earn sky-high stock multiples because their costs were flexible: if sales slowed, they could pause hiring and stay wildly profitable. Locking in **$10B–$15B+ a year** in non-cancellable rent and "pay-even-if-unused" electricity contracts flips that advantage upside down. If revenue growth slows to 5%, those fixed bills don't budge, squeezing operating margins from ~40% down toward the mid-20s.

Across the industry, four arenas show how this prison works—and where it cracks.

## 1. [Oracle] You Can't Pull Out: The Debt-Funded Builders

Unlike the Big Four, who fund their AI spending out of massive cash profits from search, social ads, and cloud software, the second-tier builders relied on borrowed money and now have no way out.

Look at **Oracle (ORCL)**. Trying to keep pace with the giants, Oracle committed **$55.7 billion** to AI data centers in fiscal 2026—more than double the year before. Because it couldn't pay that bill out of pocket, it raised **$48 billion** in twelve months ($43B in debt and $5B in new stock), pushing total corporate debt to **$124.7 billion**. That leaves Oracle carrying roughly five dollars of debt for every dollar of shareholder equity (~500% debt-to-equity, compared to ~50% at Amazon and ~30% at Microsoft), with over 83% of its assets financed by borrowing. Barclays flagged severe cash burn, the cost to insure Oracle's debt against default spiked to 2009 financial-crisis levels, and management had to promise strict limits on extra borrowing ahead of 2027 debt repayments as its credit rating hovered near junk territory.

In ancient Greece, King Croesus asked the Oracle of Delphi whether to invade Persia and was told: *"If you cross the river, a great empire will be destroyed."* He marched—and destroyed his own.

A Prisoner's Dilemma doesn't end when players suddenly see the light; it ends when someone runs out of money and gets dragged off the table. If a debt-heavy builder stumbles alone, bigger rivals will simply scoop up its customers. But if a major default reveals that used AI chips lose value faster than expected—causing private lenders to freeze credit lines across smaller data-center operators—the credit shock spreads to the whole sector.

Is Oracle an ... (pardon my pun again) oracle of other players? I don't know.

## 2. [Apple] You Decide Not to Play, but May Have to

Can a giant simply refuse to enter the cellblock—and actually make money watching its rivals bleed?

**Apple (AAPL)** kept its annual equipment and building spend around **$9 billion to $13 billion**, focusing instead on chip design, software R&D, and lightweight privacy-focused computing. As a result, it converts roughly **90% of the ~$145 billion in cash coming into the business directly into leftover cash** it can keep or return to shareholders.

Apple's restraint only looks foolish if AI models stay a rare, pricey monopoly. If AI intelligence instead becomes a cheap commodity—like electricity—**the company that owns the screen in your pocket collects the prize without paying the construction bill.** In October 2026, longtime rivals **Meta and Apple** teamed up to run Meta's free, publicly downloadable Llama AI models directly on Apple Silicon Macs. Apple gets fast, private AI on its own devices with zero per-use cloud fees, while Meta eats the multi-billion-dollar training cost. Combined with plugging in outside partners like Google and OpenAI for heavier cloud tasks, Apple has turned its rivals' $600B arms race into a free subsidy.

Sitting out still has a cost: Wall Street mocks Apple for "falling behind on AI," and there is a long-shot risk that an outside AI assistant becomes so indispensable that users stop caring which phone they hold. Still, Apple's playbook points to the only three real exits from the trap: **own the customer relationship and rent cheap AI on top**, **play competing AI providers against each other**, or **hoard cash so you can buy distressed data centers at 20 cents on the dollar if the credit boom busts.**

## 3. [Nvidia] Even the Dealer Can't Leave

There has been a running joke about Nvidia—"Nvidia puts"—meaning Nvidia has been backing multiple AI companies' loans, ranging from OpenAI, Anthropic, SB Energy (aoz: 100% shell company), CoreWeave, etc. Nvidia never just sold chips for cash; it functioned as a bank and private creditor—extending over **$230 billion** in behind-the-scenes promises to cover leases and absorb up to the **first 25% of losses** if private lenders got stuck with devalued GPUs, while pushing buyers onto an exhausting one-year chip upgrade cycle. In effect, money moved in a circle: Nvidia backed up startups and cloud buyers, who used those loans and guarantees to buy Nvidia chips, making everyone along the chain look like they were printing record sales from the exact same dollars.

So investors assumed Nvidia the dealer was untouchable, until **November 12, 2026**, when Jensen Huang abruptly resigned after 33 years as CEO and was replaced with longtime CFO **Colette Kress** right before earnings—sending shockwaves through global tech stocks.

For eleven days, the industry stared at a jarring possibility: **the ecosystem's de facto central bank had replaced its visionary salesman with an accountant.**

On the **November 17** Q3 earnings call—even as quarterly revenue more than doubled past **$115 billion**—Board Chair Harvey Jones flatly told investors the board had accepted Huang's resignation and had *no intention* of bringing him back. Yet just six days later, on **November 23, 2026**, the board reversed itself and unanimously **reinstated** Huang as CEO after public advocacy from President Trump and assurances from senior U.S. officials that his return posed no national-security barrier. Within 15 minutes, Nvidia jumped over **30%**, pulling Google, Meta, Tesla, and Asian hardware suppliers up **6% to 13%** in lockstep while everyday consumer staples stayed flat.

That 11-day round-trip exposed the true nature of the prison. First, when a board-affirmed CEO resignation gets reversed in eleven days with White House backing to protect "American technological leadership," it proves AI hardware is treated as a sovereign lifeline—meaning there is zero political permission for the spending race to slow down. Second, a market where one man's job status moves trillions of dollars across half a dozen mega-caps and Asian stock indexes in fifteen minutes isn't diversified at all; just like my `FZILX` / TSMC illusion in the prologue, owning Big Tech and global chip suppliers is really one crowded bet wearing different tickers. Finally, putting Huang back at the head of the table reverses market panic, not balance-sheet math: it restores the promoter energy behind the one-year upgrade cycle, but it doesn't erase 500% debt loads at second-tier builders or off-book lease bills coming due later this decade.

That said, I find it very comforting that the entire US and world economy now depends on one man's employment status.

## 4. [China Model Labs] A Parallel Prison

I find it a massive blind spot that commentators constantly talk about Chinese open-weight model capabilities and US lab finances, while completely ignoring the finances of China's own model labs.

By September 2026, OpenAI and Anthropic hit an annualized sales pace of **$105 billion** combined—nearly **10 times** the **$11 billion** pace of China's top six AI players combined. Look at actual profits, and the celebration disappears:

| Company | Period | Sales | Profit (or Loss) | What's Actually Happening |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **OpenAI** (US) | Q3 2026 | ~$10.0B | **-$10.9B** (operating) | Lost more than it brought in, even as sales jumped 50%. |
| **Anthropic** (US) | Q3 2026 | ~$17.0B | **-$567M** (operating); **-$23.3B** (paper net) | ~$5B in short-term compute bills erased Q2 operating profit; paper net loss distorted by investor IOU accounting. |
| **Zhipu AI / Z.ai** (CN) | H1 2026 | ~$141M–$148M | **-2.07B RMB** (~-$290M) | 26.4% gross margin; research costs alone are over 2x total sales. |
| **MiniMax** (CN) | H1 2026 | ~$116.6M | **-$358M** (net) | 17.9% gross margin; losses widening on research and talent pay. |
| **DeepSeek** (CN) | 7M 2026 | ~$70M | **~-715M RMB** (~-$100M) | Private; clever engineering keeps serving margins near 83%, pricing AI far below US peers. |
| **Moonshot AI** (CN) | 2026 | ~$1B–$2B annual pace | **Unprofitable** | Heavy training and subsidized usage ahead of planned Hong Kong IPO. |

OpenAI lost more money running its business in a single quarter than China's pure-play AI startups lose in an entire year combined. Both countries sit in parallel prisons: US labs have to pour every new dollar of sales straight into the next $10B+ server cluster or fall behind in six months, while export-restricted Chinese labs compensate with ruthless engineering efficiency and cheap open models that drag down AI pricing worldwide.

The only profitable AI players in China—**ByteDance** (~$20B first-half net profit, though margins tightened from 21–26% to 16.7% due to AI spending) and **Alibaba** (whose ~RMB 67.7B quarterly spending pushed total company leftover cash negative)—survive just like Meta and Google do: profitable legacy apps pay the rent. For standalone AI labs, the value skips right past them to **chip, memory, and power suppliers** at the bottom—and **customers** buying AI below its true cost.

That also explains the wave of cash-burning labs racing to go public—from Zhipu and MiniMax listing in Hong Kong earlier this year, to Moonshot queuing up next, to Anthropic just pulling off a record **$81.3 billion IPO** (`ANT`) at a ~$1.5 trillion valuation despite losing $567 million on operations in Q3 (huge congrats to my friends in Anthropic for the IPO!). Private backers cannot subsidize $10B-a-quarter losses forever; going public while headline sales growth still looks dazzling is how insiders cash out and pass the bill to public investors before credit markets or supply-chain bottlenecks break the illusion.

## [You] Play or Not Play: The Retail Investor's Choice

Every great infrastructure mania—from the **1840s British railway boom** to the **2000 telecom bubble** (when equipment makers like Cisco and Lucent lent billions to shaky startups just so they could buy more gear)—ran on this same script. Executives weren't stupid; they saw the overbuilding and kept spending because stopping alone meant losing the race, while continuing meant a shared crash.

Here is the massive advantage you have over every tech CEO on earth: **you get to choose whether to play.** Nobody forces an individual investor to buy into a race with no finish line—or to serve as exit liquidity when unprofitable AI startups rush to go public.

If you decide to stay out of the crossfire—or wait patiently for better prices—three simple habits keep you safe:

1. **Never Borrow to Invest**: Stay away from margin loans and speculative bets on debt-heavy AI infrastructure companies during an industry spending war.
2. **Count the Hidden Bills, Not Just Headline Profits**: Before valuing any tech giant, subtract off-book leases, locked-in power contracts, and real equipment replacement costs from its cash flow.
3. **Keep a Simple Two-Sided Defense**: In my own portfolio, I pair a heavy cushion of safe cash and short-term U.S. Treasury bills (earning around 4-5% while waiting for bargains) with physical assets that cannot be printed or disrupted by software—gold, energy reserves, and essential raw-material bottlenecks.

Once you see the AI boom as a Prisoner's Dilemma rather than a simple story about genius or hype, the daily market noise gets much easier to tune out. The executives inside the arena cannot afford to blink. As an everyday investor, your edge is simpler: stay debt-free, read past the headline cash-flow numbers, hold enough cash to stay calm when credit tightens, and put the rest of your energy into the parts of life you actually control.
