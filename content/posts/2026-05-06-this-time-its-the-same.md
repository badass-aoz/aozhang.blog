I’ve been talking to a lot of people recently — friends, coworkers, folks at the climbing gym — and there’s a shared feeling that’s hard to articulate. Not panic. More like a low hum of anxiety that won’t go away. Gas prices are up, groceries cost more than they did a year ago, the stock market swings wildly on a single headline. And underneath all of it, this nagging sense that the rules of the game might be changing in ways nobody in charge is willing to admit.

Almost exactly fifty years ago, the United States went through something remarkably similar. The way it played out should terrify anyone paying attention, because almost nobody learned the right lessons.

## The 1970s, briefly (aka the “stagflation”)

Between 1973 and 1982, the U.S. experienced stagflation: inflation and unemployment rising at the same time. Before the ’70s, most economists believed that was impossible — the Phillips curve said they were a seesaw. Stagflation broke that model.

Because of an oil shock.

OPEC imposed an embargo in 1973, quadrupling oil prices almost overnight. But the shock didn’t come from nowhere. the Suez Crisis of 1956.

When Egypt’s Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal — a critical oil transit route — Britain and France tried to take it back by force. Eisenhower refused to back them. More than that, he threatened to dump American holdings of British pounds, which would have crashed their currency. Britain withdrew humiliated. The old colonial powers lost their grip on Middle Eastern oil in a single week.

Two things came out of Suez that made the ’70s inevitable. Arab nations learned oil infrastructure was a geopolitical weapon — the canal’s closure caused fuel shortages across Europe, a proof of concept. And Nasser’s defiance electrified the Arab world, leading directly to OPEC’s formation in 1960.

The chain is almost mechanical: Suez (1956) → Arab nationalism → OPEC (1960) → Six-Day War (1967) → Yom Kippur War (1973) → oil embargo → stagflation. By 1973, they weren’t improvising. They were executing a playbook seventeen years in the making.

(The parallel to today — a Middle Eastern conflict disrupting oil markets while an empire’s influence is waning — is one I’ll leave for the reader to draw.)

The oil shock was just the match. The kindling had been building for years: loose monetary policy, deficit spending on Vietnam, and a slow erosion of confidence in the dollar after Nixon abandoned the gold standard in 1971.

## Four presidents, four wrong answers

What I find most alarming is how long it took to fix — and how many failed attempts came first.

Nixon tried wage and price controls. He literally told businesses they couldn’t raise prices. Inflation paused, shortages appeared everywhere (if you can’t raise the price of beef, ranchers sell fewer cows), and when controls lifted, inflation came back worse.

Ford tried cheerleading. His “Whip Inflation Now” campaign asked Americans to spend less, plant gardens, and wear WIN buttons. (I had fun reading those stories, albeit not those numbers.) The mild fiscal tightening, and the mildly deepened recession.

Carter and Fed Chair Arthur Burns tried gradualism. Slow, incremental rate hikes that businesses and consumers could see for what they were — half-measures. Inflation expectations became self-fulfilling: if you believe prices will be 10% higher next year, you raise your own prices now. By 1979, inflation hit 13%.

It took Volcker to do what nobody else had the stomach for. He raised the federal funds rate to nearly 20%. Unemployment peaked at 10.8%. But inflation fell from 13% to 3% by 1983, setting up one of the longest expansions in history.

A decade of politicians choosing the least painful option in the short term, which turned out to be the most painful in the long term. Nobody wanted to rip off the band-aid, so the wound festered for ten years.

## How bad it actually got

The numbers are staggering. Gas tripled from $0.36 to $1.19. Grocery prices rose 50% in half a decade. Mortgage rates hit 16-18% by 1981. The S&P 500, adjusted for inflation, lost roughly 60% between 1968 and 1982 — fourteen years of going backwards. People who did everything “right” — saved, invested, followed the financial advice of their era — got a pat on the back and a 60% haircut.

The misery index (unemployment plus inflation) peaked at 21.98 in 1980. Savings accounts earned less than inflation — you were penalized for being responsible. Steel towns in Pennsylvania and Ohio hollowed out and never came back. The Rust Belt was the visible scar of an economy that broke its compact with the middle class: work hard, show up, you’ll be okay. That stopped being true. And nobody in Washington was going to tell you — they were too busy printing WIN buttons.

There was a pervasive feeling of national decline. Vietnam had ended badly. Watergate shattered trust in institutions. The hostage crisis in Iran (the original one) played out on nightly news. America didn’t feel like the country that had put a man on the moon a decade earlier.

I think a lot of people today would recognize that feeling.

## Where the pattern rhymes

1976 doesn’t repeat, but rhymes with 2026.

**An energy shock from the Middle East.** Then: Yom Kippur War and OPEC embargo. Now: Iran conflict driving oil prices up, with fertilizer and food costs following. Same mechanism — oil spikes, everything downstream follows.

**Deficits with no credible path to discipline.** Then: Vietnam and Great Society spending. Now: interest payments on the national debt exceeding defense spending. Both parties will tell you they care about the deficit. Neither will touch the spending that causes it.

**Eroding confidence in the dollar.** Then: Nixon broke the dollar-gold peg. Now: de-dollarization accelerating as foreign governments dump Treasuries and buy gold. Different mechanism, same direction.

**The Fed’s independence under political pressure.** Then: Nixon pressured Arthur Burns to keep rates low before the 1972 election. Burns complied, and inflation got away from them. Now: the Fed Chair’s position has become a bargaining chip, with open pressure to cut rates regardless of what the data says. This is the parallel that worries me most — the Volcker solution required exactly the kind of independence that’s being traded away for short-term political convenience.

**The anxiety.** Then: gas lines, the misery index, a generation realizing next year might be worse than this year. Now: friends checking portfolios three times a day, conversations circling back to “what should I do with my money?” — not from greed, but from fear.

## Where the pattern breaks

**UAE cheated on OPEC quotas in the 1980s, and again today, but differently** They overproduced while the rest of the cartel held the line, free-riding on everyone else’s discipline. Saudi Arabia’s response in 1986 was to flood the market, crashing oil from ~$30 to ~$10. Now? They left OPEC — why miss the opportunity of selling goods when you put in so much efforts to produce them?

The take is the fragmentation of OPEC, which is doomed to produce more volatility, as we have seen already.

This matters for the dollar too. The petrodollar system relied on Saudi keeping OPEC disciplined. If members defect, that’s one fewer pillar holding dollar hegemony together. UAE has already experimented with non-USD oil trades. The cartel that once propped up the dollar system is slowly dismantling it.

**Tariffs as a new inflation channel.** The ’70s had supply shocks but free trade. Today, tariffs function as a consumption tax on top of the energy shock — an additional headwind the ’70s middle class never faced.

**AI compressing the middle.** The $60-150K bracket is squeezed from both ends: housing and healthcare compounding at 5-8% while wages grow 3-4%, and AI automating exactly the white-collar jobs in this range — skilled enough to be expensive, routine enough to be replaceable. The Rust Belt was a geographic collapse. This one could be an income-bracket collapse.

**The safety net is thinner.** Union membership dropped from ~24% in 1973 to ~10% today. Retirement savings are in equities rather than pensions. The middle class has less margin, and the assets they hold are exactly the ones that got wrecked last time.

The similarities tell us this *could* go badly. The differences tell us it could go badly in ways we haven’t seen before.

## What worked last time

Gold was the standout — but the story is more intricate than “gold went up.” Before 1971, gold was fixed at $35/oz under Bretton Woods. When Nixon abandoned the gold standard, he didn’t just devalue the dollar — he uncapped gold. The 24x run from $35 to $850 was partly a repricing: the market discovering what gold was actually worth after decades of artificial suppression.

That said, even starting from 1973 (when gold was trading freely around $100), it still 8x’d by 1980 while stocks went nowhere. The inflation-hedge thesis holds even after removing the Bretton Woods distortion. Commodities broadly — oil, agricultural, metals — did the same thing, because real assets *are* the inflation.

Real estate held up for people who already owned with fixed-rate mortgages — your $300/month payment stayed $300 while your salary eventually inflated upward. The debt got eroded for you.

Short-duration bonds and T-bills outperformed because you could roll them at rising rates. Long-term bonds got destroyed — locked into bad rates for decades.

What didn’t work: the S&P 500 (-60% real over fourteen years), cash in savings accounts (negative real returns), and the “Nifty Fifty” — the Magnificent 7 of that era — collapsed.

Today’s default retail portfolio — index funds, 60/40 allocation, high-yield savings — is basically the playbook that got wrecked last time. Every personal finance influencer will tell you to dollar-cost-average into index funds and let time do the work. That’s great advice — unless you’re entering a fourteen-year window where time works against you. Most people won’t ask. They’ll find out.

One tool exists today that didn’t in the ’70s: short-duration TIPS (funds like VTIP, holding 0-5 year maturities). The principal adjusts with CPI, so inflation doesn’t eat your value. The short duration means minimal price damage when rates rise — unlike a 30-year bond, a 2-year bond doesn’t have far to fall. Not exciting. But it’s the defensive position that would have saved a lot of retirements in 1975.

## What actually concerns me

The 1970s teach us that the worst outcome isn’t a crisis. It’s a decade of half-measures that avoid the crisis while making everything slowly worse.

Ray Dalio has a framework for this. He calls it “beautiful deleveraging” — when a country has too much debt, there are four levers: cut spending, restructure debts, print money, and redistribute through taxes. Used individually, each is destructive. Fiscal tightening alone is deflationary (Ford’s mistake). Money printing alone is inflationary. But used together, they offset each other — you reduce the deficit while the central bank prevents a deflationary collapse. Painful, but manageable.

The U.S. is currently running the exact opposite combination. Fiscal policy is loose: massive deficits, no spending discipline. Monetary policy is ambiguous: political pressure to cut rates even as inflation persists. That’s not a beautiful deleveraging. That’s the ugly version. Dalio would call it a recipe for a lost decade. I’d call it business as usual in Washington.

Volcker worked because the Fed was independent enough to impose short-term pain. Dalio’s framework explains *why*: Volcker provided tight monetary policy while Reagan (eventually) provided some fiscal discipline — not perfect, but credible enough to reset inflation expectations. My concern isn’t inflation or recession — it’s whether that coordination is even possible when rate decisions are driven by election cycles.

And the middle class has less margin than in the ’70s. Retirement savings are in equities that could correct. The S&P lost 60% real over fourteen years last time. Today’s 401(k)-dependent retirees can’t survive that.

I don’t have a tidy conclusion. But the anxiety people feel right now isn’t irrational. It’s pattern recognition. We’ve been here before, and last time, it took a decade of getting it wrong before someone got it right.

The question is whether we’re at the Nixon phase, the Ford phase, the Carter phase, or whether someone is willing to be Volcker.

I suspect we’re somewhere around Ford.
