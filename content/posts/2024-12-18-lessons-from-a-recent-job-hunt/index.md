It’s been humbling 3.5 months during my recent job hunt. I finally landed a job, and this is what I learned about interviews from a candidate’s experience.

## My Background

I brought 9 years of experience: 8 at a FAANG company and 1 at a Series D startup. After the startup’s acquisition, I took a break before job hunting. I focused mainly on Engineering Manager (EM) and Tech Lead Manager (TLM) roles in security, with one exception.

## Quick Stats

- Applied to **~60** roles (sometimes multiple in the same company)
- Got phone screens/recruiter calls from **~11** companies
- Completed full loops with **6** companies
- Received **2** offers: one TLM, one EM

## Key Learnings

### Company Expectations Vary Dramatically

I encountered vastly different emphases on:

- Technology: from securing hundreds of diverse tech stacks to focusing on one product’s privacy
- People: heavy focus on supporting people versus managing projects
- Process: operating in hyper-chaotic versus structured environments

Interview structures also varied wildly. Some memorable examples:

- An Identity Access & Management technical interview that never touched on IAM topics
- A director role requiring purely technical assessment versus a line manager role with no technical questions
- A 45-minute interview where the interviewer asked questions for 10 minutes, then flipped to Q&A – not because I failed, but because they valued hearing my questions more
- A technical interview where the interviewer decided to grill me on behaviors for 30 minutes on a whim

Unfortunately, I don’t think there’s a tried-and-true solution to any of the above.

### Question Standard Processes

Don’t take mature organizations’ processes for granted. When asked about basics like “Why require two written feedbacks before a performance improvement plan?” avoid answers like “That’s Google’s process.” Instead, explain the reasoning behind these practices and add your perspective.

### Accept the Role of Luck

Preparation helps, but external factors matter. I once had an interviewer who seemed exhausted and barely engaged with my answers. I had to actively work to regain their attention. This eventually didn’t work out. In another case, my example of adapting to a report’s communication style was interpreted as lacking opinion – the opposite of my intended demonstration of communication insight. Sometimes the dice just roll against you.

### Stay Resilient

The process gets tough. One day I felt utterly frustrated, but posts about the ongoing Israel-Arab conflicts popped into my Facebook newsfeed, so I got to remind myself how fortunate I was. This perspective helped me maintain resilience.

### Adapt Beyond STAR

The standard STAR framework (Situation, Task, Action, Result) has limitations:

- Add Learning: Include what you’d improve. One interviewer expected this after nearly every answer. I told them something that I was really proud of and couldn’t think of something that could have been done better. Later on the recruiter relayed their feedbacks that I couldn’t step back and introspect the situation.
- Handle Philosophy: STAR struggles with abstract questions like “how do you build a team”. What worked for me well was to review a book about management such as ones by Will Larson or Camille Fournier and ensure you talk through frameworks with your own examples.
- Manage Complex Stories: Not all stories has a final *Result* after an *Action*. I had a conflict resolution story with multiple twists (“STARSTARARAR”). Those are great for lunch breaks, but too long for interviews. I learned to cobble segments (“STTAAARRRR”) even though they weren’t in chronological order. This was against my habit of story telling but I just had to do it.

### Bridge Domain Knowledge Gaps

In specialist roles like security, prepare for interviewers unfamiliar with your field’s nuances. Example: I believe security professionals are less “*fungible*” than software engineers, because security domain can get really niche and there are less qualified security experts on the market than software engineers. However, I got flagged by a software engineering manager interviewer, because they believed it’s poor judgement for me to hire a specialized security expert who’s not well-rounded. Moreover, in the full interview loop for this role as a senior security engineering manager, I wasn’t asked a single security question.

### Value Connections

Network matters: 5 of my 6 full interviews came through referrals. My highest-level opportunities (both at director level) came from knowing the hiring manager. I ultimately accepted an offer from my former employer of 8 years. Much blessing from my ex-colleagues as a boomerang ❤

### Diversify Examples

While one complex situation might demonstrate multiple skills (conflict resolution, empathy, backbone), prepare various examples. Reusing the same story can suggest limited experience.

### Prepare Smart

- Do mock interviews – even with 500 interviews under my belt, I learned new perspectives
- Create a concise cheatsheet with a table of contents – long versions prove impractical mid-interview

## Aspiration

It’s been a truly humbling experience. I’ve got into a lot of self-doubt during the process, and I feel fortunate to land on a good opportunity again. In the future, I aspire to either have the opportunity find me, or be my own employer.

Never do I want to go through the same toil. And I wish you don’t have to either.
